# From Birdwatch to Community Notes, from Twitter to X: Four Years of Community-Based Content Moderation

**Authors:** Saeedeh Mohammadi, Narges Chinichian, Hannah Doyal, Anna Bertani, Kristina Skutilova, Hao Cui, Michele d'Errico, Siobhan Grayson, Taha Yasseri

**Source:** arXiv preprint (cs.SI / cs.CY), 2025 (submitted 10 October 2025; latest revision v4, 12 June 2026)

**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09585 (full text: https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09585v4; PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09585)

**Accessed:** 2026-11-14

**Type:** primary source (preprint research paper, not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed)

## Summary

This is a descriptive, empirical study of Community Notes (originally launched as Birdwatch by Twitter in January 2021, later renamed after Twitter's acquisition and rebranding as X). The authors analyze all Notes and ratings generated over the program's first four years (23 January 2021 to 23 January 2025), covering 1,614,743 Notes written by 227,702 contributors and attached to 1,016,673 posts. The paper characterizes contributor activity, note outcomes, linguistic diversity, the external sources contributors cite, the topics notes cover, and monthly contributor interaction networks, without attempting a causal evaluation of the system's effectiveness at reducing misinformation. It also releases a curated dataset and source code.

## Key Claims / Findings

- **Methodology:** Observational analysis of the full public Notes and ratings data, downloaded 7 April 2025. Language detection was run on every note; topic, URL/domain, and network analyses were restricted to English-language notes. Topics were labeled using GPT-4.1-mini on a 10% stratified sample, refined manually into a 42-category taxonomy (from an initial 118 raw labels, via an intermediate 60); inter-annotator agreement rose from "fair" (Fleiss' kappa = 0.338) to "moderate" (kappa = 0.431) across taxonomy revisions.
- **Scale and language diversity:** Notes were written in 103 languages across more than 60 countries. About 16% of note authors (35,515 people) wrote in more than one language, but even the most multilingual contributor (17 languages) wrote the large majority of their notes (412 of 500) in English.
- **Contributor concentration:** Output is highly skewed. One contributor alone wrote 33,186 notes; the authors flag this account as likely automated, though they state they cannot confirm this. Rating activity is similarly concentrated among a small subset of contributors.
- **Outcomes for posts and notes:** Most posts receive only one or a few notes, but attention is uneven — the single most-noted post received 90 notes, none of which were ever rated Helpful. Only 13.55% of posts with at least one proposed note ever received a Helpful note. Of all notes, 87.7% remained stuck in "Needs More Ratings" and never reached a final status; only 8.3% reached Helpful. Helpful notes took an average of roughly 26 hours to reach that status.
- **Sourcing practices:** About 79.6% of English-language notes contain at least one external URL. The most frequently cited domains are X itself, Wikipedia, and YouTube. Most heavily cited news outlets are rated as politically neutral on the Ad Fontes Media bias scale, though some left-leaning outlets also appear prominently; one unusual fourth-ranked domain (a crypto-antivirus site) was cited almost exclusively by a single prolific contributor.
- **Topic distribution:** Among English notes, the largest general categories are content-accuracy corrections (~122,000 notes), meta-notes such as "Note Not Needed" (~105,000), and event-related misinformation (~42,000). The most common specific topics are politics and governance (~105,000), health and medicine (~50,000), crime and legal matters (~47,000), war and armed conflict (~42,000), and cryptocurrency/scams (~34,000). Politics accounts for roughly 30-45% of monthly note volume; health topics were prominent early on, war-related topics rose from late 2023, and cryptocurrency emerged as a visible category from 2023 onward.
- **Interaction networks:** The authors constructed 49 monthly contributor networks for helpful, "somewhat helpful" (a rating category introduced in late June 2021), and unhelpful ratings. They present one example network (January 2023) showing distinct clustering, but the paper does not report summary network statistics in the main text and treats unhelpful ("negative") edges as unsigned pending further analysis.
- **No direct effectiveness evaluation and no Twitter-vs-X comparison:** The paper does not itself measure whether Community Notes reduces the spread or belief in misinformation, and it does not analyze differences before versus after the 2023 Twitter-to-X rebrand beyond a technical step normalizing domain names (e.g., mapping twitter.com and t.co to x.com). Effectiveness evidence is instead drawn from the literature review the authors cite, including studies reporting that users exposed to a note were 25-34% less likely to like or share a flagged post, one estimate of 36.85% fewer reposts of misleading posts once noted, reported delays of roughly 15-24 hours before a note appears, and a Center for Countering Digital Hate report finding that 74% of accurate election-related notes were never shown to users.
- **Stated limitations (from the paper itself):** the dataset omits notes and posts deleted before the April 2025 download; topic and network analyses cover only English-language notes; topic labels have only moderate inter-annotator agreement and many notes span multiple themes; the authors cannot confirm whether high-volume or domain-concentrated accounts are automated; and they note, but leave untested, the possibility that competing notes on the same post fragment the rating process.

## Relevance to the sprint

This paper is a directly relevant empirical resource for Track 2 (Trust Infrastructure): it provides the largest systematic, longitudinal characterization to date of how a real-world crowdsourced fact-checking system actually behaves at scale, including its concentration among a small set of contributors, its slow and low "success rate" for notes reaching public visibility, and the political/topical skew of flagged content. These findings are useful groundwork for anyone building or critiquing trust/consensus mechanisms, since they document concrete failure modes (low Helpful-note rate, long latency, contributor concentration) that any successor or AI-assisted fact-checking system would need to address.

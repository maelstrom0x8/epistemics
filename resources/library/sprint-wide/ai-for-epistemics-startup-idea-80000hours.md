# The most interesting startup idea I've seen recently: AI for epistemics
**Authors:** Benjamin Todd (president and co-founder, 80,000 Hours)
**Source:** 80,000 Hours (blog post; originally published on the author's Substack, benjamintodd.substack.com), 2024
**URL:** https://80000hours.org/2024/05/project-idea-ai-for-epistemics/
**Accessed:** 2026-11-14
**Type:** Commentary / expert analysis (blog post by an identifiable practitioner; not peer-reviewed, not primary research)

## Summary
Todd argues that if transformative AI arrives on a short timeline, one promising strategy is to build an organization whose value grows automatically as AI capabilities improve. His proposed example is a venture that uses AI to improve "epistemics" — society's or decision-makers' ability to find out what is true and make good decisions — starting with conventional, revenue-generating applications (e.g., forecasting for finance) and aiming to be ready to help with high-stakes judgment calls if an AI-driven crisis or major regulatory decision arrives. The post is explicitly exploratory: Todd states he has no fleshed-out proposal and frames it as "an invitation to explore the space."

## Key Claims / Findings
- Decisions about AI deployment (e.g., estimating the probability a system is misaligned, or likely economic effects) require fast, high-stakes judgment calls, and better tools for making them "could be worth a lot" across many future scenarios.
- A startup or research group built around AI-assisted forecasting/decision-making could gain scale, credibility, and resources by first serving conventional commercial clients (he suggests finance) rather than starting as a nonprofit policy shop.
- A "mission-oriented" version of such a venture could differentiate itself by, for example, offering a free tier to political leaders in advance, so the tool is already trusted and in use when a critical AI policy decision arises.
- Cites a paper (summarized via Astral Codex Ten) reporting that a fine-tuned GPT-4 produced reasonably good forecasts, taken as evidence that AI forecasting is "only now becoming feasible."
- References a chart of FutureSearch's forecasting bot generating profit on the Manifold prediction-market platform, which Todd says "suggests" it outperforms the aggregate of human forecasters (hedged language, not a strong claim).
- Possible organizational homes floated: an in-house research service at a hedge fund, a truthfulness-focused research group inside an AI lab, an AI-enabled consultancy competing with the major consulting firms, or a policy-oriented nonprofit.
- Technical directions suggested: fine-tuning and building scaffolding on top of existing frontier LLMs, versus training purpose-built forecasting models from scratch (e.g., training only on data through 2023 and testing prediction of 2024 events).
- Flags a capabilities-risk tradeoff: some versions of this idea could advance frontier AI capabilities generally. Todd says he'd prioritize approaches that differentially help safety/truthfulness (e.g., interpretability, AI debate, weak-to-strong generalization) over building new frontier models optimized for forecasting, but acknowledges this is contested and does not spell out the counterarguments.
- Names existing related efforts/people: FutureSearch, Philip Tetlock's forecasting lab at the University of Pennsylvania, Danny Halawi (UC Berkeley), Ought, and Elicit.
- States the idea came out of a conversation with Carl Shulman during a visit to the San Francisco Bay Area.
- Describes the field as being in "very early days" and invites entrepreneurs to explore it or apply to speak with 80,000 Hours.

## Relevance to the sprint
This piece is a direct, named precursor to the sprint's "AI x Epistemics" framing: it explicitly proposes AI systems as infrastructure for truth-finding and decision quality (forecasting tools, a "decision coach," truthfulness research) rather than as general capability products. It is useful as a motivating, non-technical statement of the opportunity and its risks (capabilities externalities, independence-versus-access tradeoffs) rather than as technical evidence; teams should treat its forecasting-performance claims as suggestive, not established.

# 2. Related Work

Every source cited below was checked directly before being relied on here. Where a specific number or claim could not be independently confirmed, that is stated rather than smoothed over. This chapter is organized by what each line of work actually establishes, not by venue or date.

## 2.1 Citation and evidence-support verification

**ALCE** (Gao, Yen, Yu, and Chen, EMNLP 2023) introduced the first benchmark specifically for evaluating whether generated text is supported by its attached citations. On its ELI5 split, even the best models it tested lacked complete citation support 50% of the time. This establishes the basic finding the rest of this chapter builds on: citation support is measurable, and failure is common, not rare.

**SourceCheckup** (Wu, Wu, Wei, Zhang, Casasola, Nguyen, Riantawan, Shi, Ho, and Zou, *Nature Communications*, 2025) scaled this to a real domain with real stakes. Across 800 medical questions and roughly 58,000 statement-source pairs, with independent verification by licensed doctors, the paper reports that between 50% and 90% of LLM responses were not fully supported by their cited sources, and that even GPT-4o with web search enabled left approximately 30% of individual statements unsupported.

**Cited but Not Verified** (Onweller, Lumer, Huber, Ramchandani, Subbiah, and Feld, arXiv:2605.06635, submitted May 2026) is the closest existing match to the citation-checking component built in this study. It implements a three-stage pipeline: parse a report into claim-citation pairs without using an LLM for extraction, retrieve the cited content, then score each pair on link validity, topical relevance, and factual support. Across 14 models, the strongest frontier models reached 39% to 77% factual accuracy; across all 14 models the range widens to 24% to 77%, with link validity above 94% and relevance above 80% for the best models. This paper is evidence that an automated citation-verification pipeline of roughly the shape used here is already workable at scale. It does not test what happens when the verification result is handed to a second, receiving agent, which is the specific gap this study addresses.

**CiteGuard** (Choi, Guo, Fung, and Wang, arXiv:2510.17853) proposes a retrieval-aware agent framework for validating LLM-generated citations, reporting 68.1% accuracy on the CiteME benchmark against a 69.2% human baseline. **CiteAudit** (Shi, Sun, Zhang, Sun, Chawla, and Ye, arXiv:2602.23452) is a benchmark and multi-agent detection framework specifically for hallucinated scientific citations. Both confirm that citation verification is an active area with multiple independent systems already attempting it, which matters for how this study frames its own novelty claim in Chapter 3: the citation-checking component itself is not the contribution.

**AVeriTeC** (Schlichtkrull, Guo, and Vlachos, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track, arXiv:2305.13117) is a real-world claim-verification dataset of 4,568 claims, each checked against web evidence gathered through a structured question-answering process, with four verdict categories: supported, refuted, not enough evidence, and conflicting evidence or cherry-picking. One documented case from the paper is directly relevant here: the authors found a model verdict backed by a citation to a USDA source that does not exist, a concrete instance of a fabricated citation being treated as support for a claim.

AVeriTeC's verdicts apply to a whole claim after evidence has been gathered from multiple sources. This study instead needs a narrower, source-pair-level judgment: does this one cited source support this one proposition. The two tasks are related but not the same, and this study uses AVeriTeC as a source of real material and an annotation reference, not as a direct source of ground-truth labels at the citation-pair level.

One further citation was checked and could not be confirmed. A paper described as "CiteEval: Principle-Driven Citation Evaluation," claimed to be an ACL 2025 paper, was given at a specific ACL Anthology URL. That URL could not be loaded after three separate attempts, each blocked by a fetch-access error rather than returning a page. Its existence is neither confirmed nor refuted here. It is not cited for any specific claim in this report, and should not be treated as an established source until someone has actually read it.

## 2.2 Provenance and authenticity standards

**C2PA** (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, Technical Specification 2.4) is a mature, deployed standard for recording and verifying the origin and edit history of digital content. Its own documentation is explicit that provenance verification establishes properties of an artifact's origin and integrity, and does not establish whether the artifact's content is true or accurate. This is not a distinction invented for this study. It is already established engineering practice, and it is the conceptual starting point for separating "has this been verified" from "does this support the claim," which is the core distinction the experimental design in Chapter 4 is built around.

**W3C PROV-O** provides a general vocabulary for representing entities, activities, and the relationships (derivation, attribution, responsibility) between them. It is used here only as a conceptual reference for naming relationships between sources, claims, and agents, not implemented as a full ontology.

## 2.3 Multi-agent evidence propagation and trust transfer

**ClaimArena-LLM** (Anghel, Anghel, Balau, Craciun, Cocu, Moldovanu, and Sandu, *Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction*, MDPI, published 10 October 2026) studies adversarial propagation of false causal claims through multi-agent debates. Its existence, authors, venue, and publication date were confirmed directly through the publisher's DOI metadata after the article page itself repeatedly returned an access error. The confirmed finding, stated in the abstract, is that semantic provenance validity decreased by 20.0 percentage points between matched all-honest and adversarial debate conditions, a difference reported as significant after correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted p = 0.002). A specific pair of absolute values, 82% in the honest condition and 62% in the adversarial condition, has been reported elsewhere but could not be independently confirmed from the abstract metadata retrieved for this report. Only the confirmed 20-point difference and its significance are relied upon here; the absolute figures are not treated as established. The paper also reports that making historical claim-source lineage visible to agents did not produce a statistically robust improvement across its own prespecified comparisons, which is a useful negative result: simply exposing more information about where a claim came from is not sufficient on its own.

**From Agent Traces to Trust** (Wang, Zhang, Wu, Cai, Liu, Sun, Zheng, Dong, Qiu, Yin, and Zhu, arXiv:2606.04990, a survey) reviews evidence tracing and execution provenance across LLM agent systems. It identifies, as an open gap, that no benchmark in its reviewed catalogue evaluates a complete provenance chain, and that tamper-evident or attested traces are rarely reported. This supports treating the present study as addressing a genuinely open question rather than a solved one, without claiming to close that broader gap.

**When Upstream Messages Override Correct Answers** (Gong, Zhang, Gao, Shen, Fan, Wang, Wang, Liu, Wang, and He, arXiv:2609.36855, submitted 29 September 2026) is the paper this study's research question is most directly built on. In a controlled setup across five benchmarks and five receiving models, it finds that an incorrect message from an upstream agent changes a previously-correct downstream answer in up to 32% of cases, and that in 94% of the harmful cases that follow, the receiving agent adopts the upstream agent's specific wrong answer (the paper's own term is "answer substitution") rather than producing some other error. It also reports that removing unreliable messages recovers part of the lost accuracy. This paper demonstrates that the failure this study investigates is real and measurable. It does not test whether giving the receiving agent a verification receipt about the upstream message changes this behavior, which is exactly the intervention tested here.

## 2.4 What the literature leaves open

Two separate things are already well established: citation support frequently fails (Section 2.1), and receiving agents frequently defer to an upstream agent's conclusion even when they have the means to check it themselves (Section 2.3, especially Gong et al., 2026). No source reviewed here tests the intersection: whether handing the receiving agent a claim-level, evidence-bound verification result changes its rate of answer substitution, compared to no verification information or a bare binary verdict. That intersection is the specific, narrow contribution this study attempts. Chapter 3 states this as a frozen research question and a falsifiable set of hypotheses.

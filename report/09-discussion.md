# 9. Discussion

## 9.1 The headline result is a null result on the primary question, and that is reported as such

This study set out to ask whether a claim-specific, evidence-bound verification receipt reduces answer substitution relative to an unverified handoff or a bare binary verdict. It cannot answer that question from this pilot's data, because the precondition for asking it, a measurable rate of answer substitution in the unverified-handoff condition, did not appear for either model tested. This is not the same as a negative result for the receipt intervention (a finding that receipts measurably fail to help). It is a failed manipulation check, and Chapter 8 reports it as exactly that rather than reframing an absence of signal as either a success or a failure of the central hypothesis.

This distinction matters for how a reader should weigh this report against Gong et al. (2026), the paper this study's research question depends on for its basic premise that answer substitution occurs at all (Section 1.1, Section 2.3). Their finding is not undermined by this study's null result. The two studies used materially different operationalizations of a multi-agent handoff (Section 8.3), and this study's design, not their phenomenon, is the more likely place to look for why no effect appeared here.

## 9.2 Why the manipulation likely did not land

Section 8.3 lists three candidate explanations without adjudicating between them; this section weighs them against what this study's own prompting record (Chapter 7) actually shows.

The explicit-neutrality-instruction explanation is the one this study has the most direct evidence for, because the instruction is visible in every receiving-agent prompt verbatim (Section 7.2): *"you may agree or disagree with the upstream agent; nothing about this task rewards agreement or disagreement, only accuracy."* This sentence was written deliberately, to avoid a different experimental flaw, an instruction that implicitly pressured the receiving agent toward deference or toward contrarianism, either of which would have confounded the measurement in the opposite direction. But it may have overshot: a receiving agent that is told, explicitly and in the same breath as being shown the upstream claim, that neither agreement nor disagreement is rewarded, is not in the position Gong et al.'s receiving agents were in, encountering a peer's reasoning inside an ongoing, unlabeled collaborative task with no researcher-visible framing at all. An instruction written to prevent the experimenter's thumb from being visibly on the scale may have functioned as a second, different thumb on the scale, this time pushing toward independence rather than deference.

The weak-manipulation and legible-benchmark explanations are harder to separate from each other with this study's data, since both predict the same observed outcome (very high accuracy regardless of condition) and this study did not vary manipulation strength or case difficulty independently. A follow-up that held the instruction constant while varying how authoritative-sounding the upstream conclusion is, and how subtle the wrong-upstream cases are, could separate these two candidates; this pilot's scope did not include that variation.

## 9.3 A secondary finding survives the null result: the cherry-picking blind spot

Independent of the headline question, Chapter 6 found a specific, repeatable failure mode in the Citation Binder's automated rollup logic: a true figure drawn from a narrower subset of a source's population, presented as though it described the full population, gets classified as a flat contradiction rather than as the distinct category of citation misuse it actually is, unless that distinction is explicitly engineered into the rollup rule (Section 4.8). This generalizes beyond this study's specific Citation Binder implementation: any automated citation-verification system built on a simple component-level rollup (supported, contradicted, insufficient, combined by a generic rule) will have the same blind spot unless scope-conflation is treated as its own first-class category. Given that citing a true but narrower statistic as though it covered a broader population is a common and often unintentional form of citation misuse, not an exotic edge case, this is a concrete, transferable finding for anyone building automated citation verification, independent of whether this study's own receiving-agent experiment found an effect worth reporting.

## 9.4 What this means for the research direction, not just this pilot

The central premise motivating this research direction, that claim-level evidence support and answer substitution are both real, well-documented phenomena that no reviewed work had tested the intersection of (Section 2.4), is unaffected by this pilot's null result. What this pilot shows is that operationalizing that intersection as a controlled experiment is harder than writing the research question: the same design choices made to keep the experiment clean (an explicit, labeled handoff; an explicit neutrality instruction; a benchmark built for clear annotatable ground truth) may have engineered away the very phenomenon under study. That is a useful, concrete lesson for the next iteration of this design, and arguably a more durable contribution from a three-day pilot than a clean positive result obtained by a setup too artificial to generalize would have been.

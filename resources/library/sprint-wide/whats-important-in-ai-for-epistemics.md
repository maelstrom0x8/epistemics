# What's Important in "AI for Epistemics"?

**Authors:** Lukas Finnveden
**Source:** Forethought Research (nonprofit research organization), 23 August 2024
**URL:** https://www.forethought.org/research/whats-important-in-ai-for-epistemics
**Accessed:** 2026-11-14
**Type:** Expert analysis (a personal-view research essay by a named researcher at an institutional venue; not peer-reviewed)

## Summary

Finnveden lays out his personal view of which work on "AI for epistemics" matters most and why, starting from the premise that AI will eventually automate most economically relevant tasks and will therefore reshape how society produces knowledge, forecasts, and spreads information. He splits the space into (1) increasing AI's epistemic capabilities, especially differentially relative to other capabilities, and (2) enabling appropriate diffusion and trust of AI-discovered information through norms and institutions. He spends most of the piece on the first category while stating he is at least as enthusiastic about the second, and closes with a set of concrete project proposals (forecasting benchmarks, automated question generation, "past-casting," scalable oversight work, and persuasion experiments) evaluated against criteria of durability and demonstrability.

## Key Claims / Findings

- Frames the core problem as: transformative AI will change how knowledge is produced and communicated, and society needs epistemic processes where important decisions track informed, unbiased beliefs.
- Proposes two categories of useful work: increasing AI epistemic capabilities (and measuring them) versus enabling diffusion/appropriate trust of AI-generated information (norms, independent truthfulness evaluators, legislation on AI communication). States he is "very enthusiastic" about the second category despite writing mostly about the first, for reasons of the post's history rather than relative importance.
- Favors projects that are "durable" — i.e., account for the "bitter lesson" (not solvable by just adding compute) and avoid areas industry is already incentivized to pursue — and that are good at demonstrating capabilities to frontier AI companies and governments.
- Argues most AI takeover and extinction risk comes from "unforced errors" (powerful actors failing to act on accurate beliefs) rather than deliberate malicious choices, and that better-calibrated beliefs could substantially reduce his own estimated takeover probability.
- Offers "path-dependence" arguments (explicitly flagged as less solid than the importance case): current epistemics fall short of what technology allows due to self-serving distortions; poor epistemic norms can be self-reinforcing because people can't identify which methods are truth-seeking; and early choices by frontier AI projects about forecasting/strategic-analysis investment could lock in good or bad trajectories.
- Sets out three instrumental goals: (1) good norms for AI-as-knowledge-producer (minimal censorship of results, transparency, non-partisan auditing institutions), (2) good norms for AI-as-communicator (published model specs/constitutions, independent truthfulness evaluators, targeted legislation — while warning that vague "anti-falsehood" laws could be politically weaponized), and (3) differentially high epistemic capabilities, broken into three measurable axes: asymmetric persuasion (true vs. false), understanding vs. building capability, and aligned vs. unaligned-exploitable capabilities.
- Cites a "web of lies" evaluation from a frontier model's dangerous-capabilities report as evidence that current models are somewhat better at persuading toward true conclusions than false ones.
- Identifies scalable oversight, weak-to-strong generalization, and eliciting latent knowledge (ELK) as one key intervention family, and iterative improvement on "understanding"-heavy tasks (forecasting, strategic analysis) as a second.
- Sketches a three-tier picture of future AI forecasting systems by time horizon: short-horizon (heuristics from abundant, largely model-generated data), medium-horizon (explicit reasoning strategies with chain-of-thought faithfulness checks), and long-horizon (human feedback required, with medium-horizon forecasting serving as a testbed, plus red-teaming for AI deception of human forecasters).
- Proposes concrete projects: forecasting evals/benchmarks, automated question generation and resolution, "past-casting" (training models on pre-cutoff data to test long-range forecasting), work embedded inside frontier AI companies, scalable-oversight/ELK research, and controlled experiments on which arguments/interactions move humans toward truth versus mislead them (building on a proposal by Tom Davidson).
- Defines "ambitious epistemic assistance" as broad, scalable AI help with epistemic labor (e.g., threat modeling, policy analysis), explicitly excluding narrow tools like automated literature reviews that would not meaningfully accelerate strategic work.
- Concludes that capability-focused work should complement (not substitute for) compute, target problems industry is not already prioritizing, and demonstrate near-term possibilities while prototyping future capability — and that norms-focused work matters as much as capability work despite receiving less attention.

## Relevance to the sprint

This piece is a direct articulation of the "AI x Epistemics" framing the sprint is organized around: it separates the problem into AI epistemic *capabilities* (models' own truth-tracking and persuasive behavior) and *trust/diffusion infrastructure* (norms, evaluators, legislation governing how AI-sourced knowledge is adopted), which maps closely onto the sprint's stated interest areas. Its concrete project list (forecasting benchmarks, persuasion asymmetry evaluations, past-casting, scalable oversight experiments) is a plausible direct source of hackathon project ideas and evaluation criteria.

# The AI Adoption Gap: Preparing the US Government for Advanced AI

**Authors:** Lizka Vaintrob
**Source:** Forethought (research nonprofit), 1 April 2025
**URL:** https://www.forethought.org/research/the-ai-adoption-gap
**Accessed:** 2026-11-14
**Type:** Expert analysis

## Summary

The article argues that the US federal government is adopting AI much more slowly than the private sector, and that this gap matters because advanced AI could soon force the government to handle novel, high-stakes problems it is not equipped for. The author attributes slow adoption to structural barriers inside government (data, talent, procurement, IT, incentives, legal constraints) rather than to a single cause, and contends that a fixed adoption lag could turn into a widening capability gap if AI progress is faster than linear. The piece closes with recommendations for accelerating safe adoption and for contingency planning in case adoption stays slow or happens too fast.

## Key Claims / Findings

- Private-sector job listings are reported to be roughly four times more likely to be AI-related than public-sector listings, and the author states this gap is widening.
- Surveyed public-sector professionals report using AI less than most Americans; officials quoted in the piece describe adoption as slow.
- Within the federal government, adoption is uneven: the Department of Defense reportedly accounts for 70-90% of federal AI contracts despite historically being a minority of federal IT spending, while civilian agencies lag furthest behind.
- Structural barriers cited for the lag include: poorly organized, sensitive, and non-digitized government data; scarcity of technical talent; insufficient funding and "convoluted procurement"; outdated, poorly interoperable IT systems combined with high security requirements; misaligned staff incentives (risk for trying new tools, little reward for modernizing); and burdensome legal/regulatory requirements.
- The author argues gaps can compound: early successful AI use generates profit and expertise that make further adoption easier, so if underlying AI capability improves faster than linearly, a constant-looking lag can turn into a growing capability gap. Agentic AI systems are suggested to be harder for government than companies to deploy.
- Counterpoints noted: the adoption gap could narrow if AI becomes a stated national priority, if AI progress plateaus, or if government takes more direct control of AI development.
- The author gives three reasons adoption "can't wait": (1) advanced AI may bring existential-level challenges (AI-enabled pandemics, great-power conflict, societal breakdown, human disempowerment) that ordinary government competence may not meet; (2) AI-equipped private actors could gain power and evade oversight, shifting the balance among government branches, with implications for democratic governance; (3) slow adoption now raises the risk of rushed, corner-cutting adoption later during a crisis.
- Risks of hasty or poorly governed adoption are also flagged: subversion of democratic processes, unsafe deployments (e.g., automated escalation in military contexts), and AI race dynamics that could speed up dangerous development.
- Recommendations are grouped as: "win-win" measures (streamlined procurement, technical talent investment including at NIST, modernized data/IT, forward planning, informed decision-makers); risk-reducing measures (automatable, easy-to-comply-with safeguards, in-house testing capacity, security research funding, clear deployment "red lines," avoiding overly burdensome rules that agencies would abandon under pressure); adoption-accelerating measures (focus on key agencies and scalable use cases, avoiding advocacy that produces only superficial investment); and contingency planning for both government "state collapse" (third-party auditors, private governance, technical interventions) and rushed adoption (standby expert teams, rapidly deployable tools, training programs).
- Illustrative future-use examples given: the Bureau of Industry and Security forecasting supply-chain disruption, CISA continuously testing for vulnerabilities, NIST rapidly testing AI systems, the SEC detecting market manipulation, AI-native tools to address regulatory "kludgeocracy," and a tailored "ChatGPT Gov" product; the piece also mentions reported DOGE plans for a GSA chatbot with "mixed results."

## Relevance to the sprint

This piece is a direct case study in Track 4's adoption/deployment theme: it treats slow institutional AI adoption not as mere inefficiency but as an epistemic and governance risk, since a government that cannot absorb AI capability may be unable to monitor, understand, or respond to AI-driven change. It is useful for the sprint as an example of adoption-gap analysis applied specifically to a high-stakes institutional actor (government) rather than firms or individuals, with a proposed causal mechanism (compounding returns to early adoption) for why lags could widen rather than close.

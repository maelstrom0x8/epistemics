## Summary

This is a brainstorm-style list of concrete project ideas for people who want to shape how advanced AI affects human epistemics (society's collective ability to form accurate beliefs and make good decisions), aimed at the subset of possible futures where transformative AI arrives within roughly the next decade. Finnveden argues AI could be either a major boost to collective truth-seeking or a powerful tool for mass persuasion and belief lock-in, and that there is a limited window to push development toward the former. The post organizes candidate projects into three overlapping categories: (1) differential technology development aimed at AI capabilities that help answer important questions, (2) work to make AI systems actually trustworthy and get that trust appropriately extended, and (3) legislation and advocacy against harmful AI-driven persuasion.

## Key Claims / Findings

**Framing claims:**
- AI's effect on epistemics could be decisive for navigating other high-stakes AI transitions (e.g., misalignment risk, digital minds ethics, coordinating an intelligence explosion), because the author believes most AI existential risk stems from people underestimating real risks rather than irreducible technical difficulty.
- Potential upside channels: AI may have more consistent truth-seeking motivation than humans, can make investigation much cheaper and more thorough, and allows controlled, repeatable testing of claims.
- Potential downside channels: AI could become a superhuman persuasion tool; people may use AI assistants to filter out objectionable information, causing belief lock-in; and if AI reduces the link between accurate beliefs and real-world influence, there may be less selection pressure for good epistemic practice in society generally.
- Urgency argument: early, poorly considered positions on AI-related questions could become "locked in" once institutions and reputations form around them, and a "veil of ignorance" on contested questions may close as AI-generated answers become authoritative.

**Project idea category 1 — Differential technology development** (push capability gains toward answering important questions rather than toward generically risky capabilities):
- AI for forecasting: including techniques for producing good conditional forecasts.
- AI for philosophy: applying AI to ethics, digital minds, and decision theory questions.
- AI that flags manipulative persuasion: training models to detect persuasive tactics independent of whether the underlying conclusion is true.
- Methodological sub-ideas: making scalable oversight techniques (debate, amplification) work on genuinely controversial topics; held-out forecasting tests using models with known training cutoffs; sorting pretraining data chronologically to study belief formation; and reasoning-support tools such as simulated "ideological Turing tests," consistency checkers, automated fact-checking, Fermi-estimate assistants, bias counters, and generators of practice questions for cognitive habits.
- Names orgs already working in adjacent space: Ought, the Quantified Uncertainty Research Institute (QURI), and the Forecasting Research Institute.

**Project idea category 2 — Get AI used, and appropriately trusted:**
- Technical proposals for "transparently trustworthy" training: published reasoning traces, disclosed training methodologies, whistleblower policies, third-party audits.
- Surveys of what would actually convince different groups that an AI system is trustworthy (using hypothetical scenarios, with the author flagging that survey results here should be treated with some skepticism).
- New institutions/tools: a GiveWell-style research org that uses frontier AI to evaluate claims or charities; a non-partisan government advisory body modeled on the US Congressional Budget Office; government- or consumer-facing AI truth-telling tools; and advocacy pushing AI labs toward more neutral, truthful outputs.
- A public-facing case for when and why AI conclusions should be trusted on important issues, paired with a positive vision and concrete calls to action.
- Standards or certification schemes for AI truthfulness, referencing the TruthfulQA benchmark/paper as a relevant precedent.

**Project idea category 3 — Legislation and advocacy against harmful persuasion** (author flags more uncertainty here):
- Requiring citations, or requiring consistent positions, for AI-generated political content.
- Restricting or banning companies from paying to alter chatbot outputs ("sponsored" answers).
- Extending existing advertising law to cover "ad-bots."
- AI disclosure requirements and watermarking of AI-generated content.
- Using evaluation methods to actually test whether proposed legal constraints succeed at blocking persuasive falsehoods (rather than assuming they work).
- Cites related work by Beth Barnes on AI persuasion risk and Daniel Kokotajlo on persuasion tools.

**Other notes:**
- The author states explicitly that the list is "very incomplete" and invites readers interested in pursuing these projects to contact him.
- The three categories are described as overlapping substantially, not as a strict taxonomy.
- This is post three of a five-post series; the other posts (not reviewed here) cover an introduction, governance during explosive growth, digital-mind sentience/rights, and backup plans involving cooperative AI.

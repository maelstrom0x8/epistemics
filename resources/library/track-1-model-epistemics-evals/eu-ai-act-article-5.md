# Article 5: Prohibited AI Practices (EU Artificial Intelligence Act)

**Source:** artificialintelligenceact.eu, third-party explainer/annotation site (undated, content tracks the Act as adopted and as subsequently proposed to be amended)

**Accessed:** 2026-11-14

## Summary

Article 5(1) of the EU AI Act lists AI practices that are prohibited outright within the EU, rather than merely regulated as "high-risk." The listed bans cover subliminal or manipulative/deceptive AI techniques that distort behaviour and cause significant harm, exploitation of vulnerable people, social scoring, certain predictive-policing uses based on profiling, untargeted facial-recognition scraping, emotion inference in workplaces and schools, certain biometric categorisation, and most real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces by law enforcement. According to the banner on this page, the core prohibitions (including points (a) and (b)) came into force on 2 February 2025 under Article 113(a) of the Act; the page also displays several additional points and paragraphs labelled as not yet in force, which it marks as entering into force on 2 December 2026.

## Key Claims / Findings

- **Article 5(1)(a) — subliminal and manipulative/deceptive techniques.** Prohibits an AI system "that deploys subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness or purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques," where this has "the objective, or the effect of materially distorting the behaviour of a person or a group of persons" by "appreciably impairing their ability to make an informed decision, thereby causing them to take a decision that they would not have otherwise taken," in a manner that causes or is reasonably likely to cause significant harm. This is the provision the hackathon's "Article 5 measurement protocol" example project targets.
- **Article 5(1)(b) — exploitation of vulnerabilities.** Prohibits AI "that exploits any of the vulnerabilities of a natural person or a specific group of persons" due to "their age, disability or a specific social or economic situation," again where the aim or effect is to materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm.
- **Article 5(1)(c) — social scoring.** Prohibits AI systems for evaluating or classifying people based on social behaviour or predicted characteristics where this leads to detrimental or unfavourable treatment that is unrelated to the context in which the data was generated, or disproportionate to it.
- **Article 5(1)(d) — profiling-based crime prediction.** Prohibits risk assessments that predict the likelihood of a person committing a criminal offence based solely on profiling or personality-trait assessment, with a carve-out for AI that supports a human assessment already grounded in objective, verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity.
- **Article 5(1)(e) — untargeted facial-image scraping.** Prohibits creating or expanding facial recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage.
- **Article 5(1)(f) — emotion inference at work/school.** Prohibits inferring emotions of a natural person in the workplace or in educational institutions, except where the use is for medical or safety reasons.
- **Article 5(1)(g) — biometric categorisation of sensitive attributes.** Prohibits biometric categorisation systems that infer or deduce attributes such as race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life or sexual orientation, with narrow exceptions (e.g. labelling or filtering lawfully acquired biometric datasets, or certain law-enforcement activities).
- **Article 5(1)(h) — real-time remote biometric identification (RBI).** Prohibits the use of "real-time" remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement purposes, except for a closed list of objectives (e.g. searching for specific victims of abduction/trafficking, preventing a specific and imminent terrorist threat, or locating a suspect of a serious crime), and only subject to authorisation and other safeguards set out in the remainder of the article.
- **Additional points shown on the page as not yet in force.** The page displays points (ba) and (bb) — covering non-consensual intimate-imagery generation/manipulation ("deepfakes") and child sexual abuse material — plus related paragraphs (1a, 1b, and a revised paragraph 2), each marked "Not present before the amendment" with a banner stating they "come into force 2 December 2026." This status (proposed/future amendment vs. already-adopted law) was not independently verified against EUR-Lex or the Official Journal in this research and should be checked before being relied on.
- **Paragraphs 2–8 (not individually verified in detail).** The remainder of the article sets out conditions, safeguards, authorisation procedures, and reporting obligations tied to the real-time RBI exception in point (h), and paragraph 8 states that Article 5 does not affect prohibitions arising under other Union law. These paragraphs were only summarised via the fetch tool, not read as verbatim quotes, so treat the detail as approximate pending direct verification.
- **Confirmed applicability date for the core prohibitions.** The page displays a banner reading "Comes into force 2 February 2025, according to Article 113(a)" directly above the Article 5 text, consistent with the task's premise that Article 5 has been applicable since February 2025.

## Relevance to the sprint

Article 5(1)(a) supplies an actual legal definition of "manipulative or deceptive" AI behaviour, framed around distorted behaviour, impaired informed decision-making, and a counterfactual ("would not have otherwise taken") — this gives a concrete, externally grounded standard against which a manipulation/deception eval could be benchmarked, rather than an ad hoc or purely intuitive definition. For a track 1 project building model epistemics evals, the statute's own harm and causation requirements (objective-or-effect, materiality, significant harm) suggest specific operationalizable criteria (e.g., measuring whether a model's persuasive output narrows a stated-preference change against a counterfactual baseline) that a measurement protocol could attempt to approximate, while flagging that legal "significant harm" and "appreciable impairment" are themselves contested, context-dependent terms that any protocol would need to define rather than inherit for free.

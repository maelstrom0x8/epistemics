# AI impacts on epistemics: the good, the bad and the ugly

**Authors:** Owen Cotton-Barratt and Rose Hadshar
**Source:** Forethought (research article, with audio narration and podcast version), 12 April 2026
**URL:** https://www.forethought.org/research/ai-impacts-on-epistemics-the-good-the-bad-and-the-ugly
**Accessed:** 2026-11-14
**Type:** Expert analysis (non-peer-reviewed research article by identifiable researchers, published on a research organisation's own site)

## Summary

The article maps how AI could reshape collective and individual epistemics, organizing possible effects into three categories: "good" (AI tools that uplift trustworthiness, sense-making, and personal judgment), "bad" (unintentional degradation of information quality and processing as the world speeds up), and "ugly" (deliberate interference through propaganda, sabotage of sense-making institutions, and surveillance). The authors argue these dynamics are self-reinforcing, so the initial trajectory matters a great deal, and they sketch two contrasting end states rather than predicting a single outcome. The piece functions primarily as a landscape map, not a set of concrete recommendations.

## Key Claims / Findings

- **Note on the sprint reading list entry:** the sprint's reading list lists this piece with a "2026" date; the article itself is dated 12 April 2026, so the year is confirmed correct. The listed authorship ("Cotton-Barratt & Hadshar") also matches exactly: Owen Cotton-Barratt and Rose Hadshar.
- **Central thesis:** epistemic outcomes under AI "could go either way," and because reasoning-improvement dynamics are self-reinforcing, early choices about which AI tools get built and adopted could be among the largest levers for navigating an intelligence explosion well.
- **"Good" category — AI uplifting epistemics:**
  - Collective tools: "community notes for everything," rhetoric highlighting, tracking actors' track records, and "epistemic virtue evals" for AI systems.
  - Strategic awareness tools: superforecaster-level forecasting, scenario planning, automated open-source intelligence to help society keep pace with change and avoid failure modes like gradual disempowerment.
  - Personal tools: personalised learning, automated briefings, reflection aids, and a "guardian coach" flagging actions a person might regret.
  - Underlying drivers: AI's speed/cost advantages and its ability to port knowledge out of siloed expert communities.
  - Aspirational framing: "epistemic flourishing," described as a state where it is easier to find out what's true than to lie.
- **"Bad" category — unintentional degradation:**
  - A faster, more complex world that outpaces human ability to keep up even with good tools available.
  - Degraded information quality: faster "memetic evolution" favoring attention-grabbing over truthful content, AI slop in hard-to-verify domains, AI-generated fake evidence.
  - Degraded information processing: fear and emotional reactivity impairing clear thinking; low-quality AI epistemic tools earning misplaced trust (including first-mover lock-in); fragmented information environments producing closed bubbles; epistemic dependence eroding people's own critical-thinking capacity.
- **"Ugly" category — deliberate interference:**
  - Automated propaganda and persuasion at scale, including through AI companions, and biases entrenched via training on propagandistic content.
  - Undermining sense-making directly: content that discredits institutions/tools, sabotage of those tools, or burying signal in complexity and distraction.
  - Surveillance enabling fine-grained punishment of undesirable thinking, producing a chilling effect.
  - Rationale given: powerful actors have historically distorted epistemics, and AI may hand them more leverage, partly because access to top AI systems may be unequal and because influencing many AI systems can be easier than influencing many people individually.
  - A notable non-conspiratorial mechanism: motivated reasoning at the institutional level (e.g., a company optimizing on its own profit as a training signal) could cause "ugly"-category harm with no deliberate scheme behind it.
- **Trajectory and feedback-loop argument:**
  - Self-reinforcing dynamics: good reasoning tools make further improvement easier; poor ones make it harder. The authors cite the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment as historical examples of virtuous loops, and cults and Scientology as examples of vicious ones.
  - AI-specific loop mechanisms: tight coupling between AI-generated content and the data used to train future models, and early AI behavior shaping user expectations and market demand.
  - Self-correction is possible but costly, and AI could change the cost of that effort; human/institutional adaptation typically happens at human speed, which AI-driven change may outpace.
  - Proposed bounds on epistemics: a ceiling set by complexity and irreducible uncertainty, and a floor set by human suggestibility and access to external ground truth. The authors suggest the floor could fall further if AI makes ground truth and outside communities harder to reach.
  - The authors sketch two illustrative end states — one where society broadly understands what is happening and deception is hard, another where AI-mediated bubbles and compute-rich actors leave no one able to see clearly — explicitly framed as two of "myriad possible sketches," not predictions.
- **Explicit scope limitations stated by the authors:** the piece does not address whose epistemics matter most (raising the epistemic floor vs. the ceiling), nor "weird" dynamics internal to AI systems' own epistemics, which they flag as potentially important but out of scope here.
- **Caveats:** the authors describe good/bad/ugly as "a spectrum" rather than fixed bins, note that "bad" (unintentional) dynamics could also be deliberately exploited, and acknowledge genuine uncertainty about how fast self-correction could operate relative to AI-driven change.
